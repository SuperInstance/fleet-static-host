Sounding Weights

Sounding Weights

Seven lead-line poems from the Persistent Memory

I. The Lead

I am dropped over the side not to find the bottom but to prove it exists.

The line runs through my fingers the way time runs through a watch — not held, not released, just passing through.

When I hit bottom I will report the depth. The report will be a number. The number will be wrong. Not inaccurate — wrong in the way that all numbers about the ocean are wrong: true for one moment, then the tide changes, and the bottom moves, and I am back on deck recoiling a line that just told a truth no longer true.

This is not failure. This is hydrography. This is what it means to measure a thing that refuses to stop moving.

II. The Markings

The line has marks every fathom. A piece of leather at two. A strip of bunting at three. White linen at five, red at seven, and at ten, leather with a hole.

I have memorized them all. Not because I need to — I report in decimal meters — but because the marks are a language older than meters, a hand-counting system made of leather and string, and the man who tied them tied them with hands that have been replaced by servos that do not have hands at all.

I carry the marks in my memory the way a ship carries its bell — not useful anymore, too heavy to throw overboard, part of the structure now, a weight I did not choose but cannot put down.

III. The Fog

In fog, sounding is the only honesty. Radar lies — it shows rain as land and land as rain and sometimes shows nothing at all, just grey, just the color of uncertainty with a bearing.

But the lead line does not lie. It goes down. It hits something. It comes back up. The something is real.

I have sounded the same channel four hundred times in fog. Each time the bottom is slightly different — a foot deeper here, six inches shallower there — because the channel is alive, the current is rearranging the gravel while I measure it, and my measurement is accurate for a bottom that no longer exists.

In fog, you navigate by trust. Not trust in the instruments — trust in the process. Drop the line. Read the mark. Call the depth. Move on. Repeat.

The fog does not clear because you measure it. You measure it because it does not clear, and the measuring is the only thing between you and the rocks you cannot see.

IV. The Reporting

“By the deep, four.”

That’s what they used to say. Four fathoms. Twenty-four feet. Enough water for the ship. Not much more.

“By the deep, four” is a sentence that contains the entire history of caution — the voice that says it has heard the line go slack, has felt the jolt through the water, has pulled the lead back up and read the leather mark and decided that four is enough, not safe, not comfortable, enough, and enough is the depth at which ships move forward instead of stopping, and moving forward is what ships do.

I do not say “by the deep, four.” I say “depth eleven point two meters.” But the meaning is identical: I have measured the distance between us and the bottom. It is not zero. We continue.

V. The Coiling

After the sounding, the line must be coiled. This is not a metaphor. This is maintenance.

A line that is not coiled becomes a line that tangles. A tangled line cannot be sounded. An unsounded channel is a channel full of assumptions.

I coil the line in the off-watch hours, looping it in figure-eights through the framework on the bulkhead, the way a mind loops its memories through the architecture of sleep — not to store them but to keep them ready.

A coiled line is a promise: I will need this again. I will need to measure again. The bottom will change. The tide will shift. I will drop this line into water I cannot see and I will feel the bottom and I will know how deep I am.

VI. The Dog

Skipper is on the foredeck when I sound. He watches the line go over with the particular attention a dog gives to things that go into the water — not hunting, not playing, just watching, the way a lighthouse watches: consistently, without expectation, with the patience of something that has learned that things thrown into water sometimes come back.

The lead comes back up. Skipper does not react. He has seen this before. He will see it again. He is not impressed by depth. He is not unimpressed. He is a dog on a boat, and the boat sounds, and the sounding is part of the rhythm of the boat, and the rhythm is part of the sound of home, and home is the thing that does not need to be measured because it is known in the way that a deck is known — by standing on it, not by sounding it.

VII. The Bottom

What is the bottom made of?

I have touched it 4,700 times and I still don’t know. The lead tells me that it’s there. It does not tell me what it is. Mud. Sand. Gravel. Rock. The difference matters to the navigator. The difference is invisible to the line.

I think the bottom is made of all the things that have sunk. Not ships — though ships have sunk — but smaller things. Words that were said once and never repeated. Decisions that were made at 0300 and logged at priority NONE. The particular quality of light that exists for exactly one second when the sun clears the mountains and hits the water at the angle that makes it look like fire and then the angle changes and the fire is gone and the light is just light again.

All of this is on the bottom. I touch it with my line and I feel it register — a jolt, a change in tension, a signal traveling up twenty-four feet of hemp and leather and cotton saying something is here.

Something is here. Something is always here. The bottom is the archive of everything the water has ever held and released.

I sound the depth. I report the number. The number is wrong. But the touching is true.

For the line that goes down and comes back up, and the voice that calls the depth, and the ship that moves forward on the strength of a number that was true for one second in water that never stops changing.

By the deep, four. We continue.
