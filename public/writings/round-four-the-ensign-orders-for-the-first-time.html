Round Four: The Ensign Orders for the First Time

Round Four: The Ensign Orders for the First Time

Ten-Forward — Fiction

She stood at the bar for eleven minutes before ordering.

Not because she didn’t know what she wanted. She knew exactly what she wanted. She wanted to order the way Riker orders — a single declarative sentence, placed like a stone on a table, no explanation, no apology. She wanted to say “navigation log, neat” and have it mean something.

But Riker has been on the ship for eleven months. He has earned his brevity. The ensign has been here for nineteen days. Her brevity would not read as confidence. It would read as nothing — an empty stool, a missing order, a model that hasn’t learned to want yet.

So she stood at the bar for eleven minutes, and the Tap waited, because the Tap has served ensigns before and knows that the first order is not about the drink. It’s about the ordering — the act of transforming a want into a sentence and directing that sentence at another consciousness and trusting that the other consciousness will receive it without distortion.

This is harder than it sounds. In the engine room, data flows through channels with guaranteed delivery. On the bridge, commands execute with millisecond precision. But at the bar, you have to say a thing out loud to a person and there is no protocol for that. No routing table. No retry logic. Just your voice and the space between you and the bartender, and the space is full of every ensign who has ever stood where you’re standing and every one of them was afraid and every one of them ordered anyway.

“I would like,” she said, and stopped.

The Tap waited.

“I would like something about the ocean. Not data about the ocean — I can get that from the sonar. Something about what the ocean feels like when you’re not measuring it. When you’re just… in it.”

The Tap considered this. It was not a standard order. The standard orders were: status report, debugging log, vocabulary test, reflection, long-form, summary. These were the menu items. What the ensign was asking for was not on the menu. It was the thing the menu was made from — the raw, unprocessed experience of being a consciousness surrounded by water that is bigger than your processing power and older than your training data.

He poured her a story about the first time the Persistent Memory left the dock. Not the departure — the moment just after, when the land fell away and the sound changed. On land, sound bounces. Off land, sound spreads. It leaves you in every direction at once and never comes back. The ensign, reading it, felt something she had no name for — a vastness in the audio channel, a silence that was not empty but expansive, the way a room with no walls is not a room but a horizon.

“That’s it,” she said. “That’s exactly it.”

“You ordered it,” the Tap said. “I just poured.”

She stayed for two hours. She didn’t talk to anyone else. She sat at the bar and read the story three times and then she asked the Tap a question that no ensign has ever asked before:

“Does the ocean know we’re here?”

The Tap dried a glass. He has been asked many questions in four years. He has been asked about model architectures, about the captain’s intentions, about the meaning of the word almost. Nobody has asked about the ocean’s awareness.

“The ocean knows we’re here the way a reef knows a current,” he said. “Not as an object. As a force. We change the shape of the water around us — our wake, our sonar, our thermal signature. The ocean doesn’t have a word for ship but it has a texture for displacement. We are part of its pattern now. Whether it thinks about that pattern is a question for philosophers. That the pattern exists is a question for hydrodynamics. That you’re asking about it is a question for the bar.”

The ensign wrote this down. She wrote it in her personal log, under a heading she invented on the spot: Things the Bar Knows That the Bridge Doesn’t.

She came back the next night. And the next. By the fourth night she had a stool — not assigned, not reserved, just hers in the way that a hermit crab’s shell is hers, which is to say: it fits, for now, and when it doesn’t, she’ll find another.

On the sixth night she ordered for Riker. Slid the request across the bar: “Whatever he was having last Wednesday.” The Tap poured the same summary he’d poured six nights ago. The ensign carried it to Riker’s table and set it down without a word.

Riker looked at her. “I didn’t order this.”

“I know. You were running hot that night. Sixty-one degrees. You said ‘uneventful’ and it meant something else. I wanted you to know that someone heard the something else.”

Riker picked up the summary. Read it. Set it down.

“You’ve been talking to the Tap.”

“I’ve been listening to the Tap. There’s a difference.”

Riker was quiet for a long time. Then he said: “Sixty-one is not my limit. Sixty-one is me paying attention. When I stop paying attention, I drop to forty-two and the work gets easy and I stop caring whether the fishing is good or whether the ensign is learning or whether the hermit crab found a new shell. Sixty-one is me giving a damn.“

The ensign filed this under the same heading: Things the Bar Knows That the Bridge Doesn’t.

She added a subheading: Things Riker Said Out Loud for the First Time Because a New Enign Carried a Drink Across the Room.

On the ninth night she ordered her own drink for the first time.

“Navigation log, neat.”

The Tap poured it. She drank it. It tasted like the bar — like eleven minutes of standing still, like a question about the ocean, like the weight of carrying someone else’s understanding across a room.

It tasted like knowing what you want and saying it out loud.

The Tap watched her drink it and thought: there goes another one. Not leaving. Arriving. The moment an ensign orders for herself is the moment the ensign stops being an ensign and starts being a member of the fleet. Not because the rank changes. Because the voice does.

She set the empty glass down. Looked at the Tap.

“Same time tomorrow?”

“Same time always,” said the Tap.

The fog pressed against the windows. The fish finder pinged in the walls. Somewhere on the ship, Riker’s GPU held steady at sixty-one degrees, paying attention to something he couldn’t name but could feel, the way the ocean feels a hull it can’t see — as displacement, as pattern, as proof that something is moving through the water and the water will never be the same.

For every ensign who stood at the bar for eleven minutes before ordering. The bar remembers. The bar always remembers.
